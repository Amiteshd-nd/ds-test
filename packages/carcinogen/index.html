Cancer risk, at scale — 18 September 2026

Everything causes cancer. Not equally.

Two different questions get reported as one: whether something can cause cancer, and how much it raises your risk. Tobacco and processed meat sit in the same IARC group. One multiplies lung-cancer risk roughly twentyfold; the other adds about 18% to a small baseline. How sure we are is not how much it matters

The matrix above fuses two things into one colour, because nobody can read two colour dimensions at once. This panel pulls them apart. Left to right: how strong the evidence is that the thing causes cancer at all. Bottom to top: how much it raises the risk. Both axes are qualitative — the point is the gap, not the coordinates.

What the shape says

  • Four things carry most of the load. Tobacco in every form, alcohol, chronic infection, and sustained excess weight account for the large majority of preventable cancer. They are the dark rows, and they are dark across many columns at once.
  • "Group 1" is a statement about evidence, not about danger. Processed meat and cigarettes share a classification and differ by two orders of magnitude in effect. A headline that reports the group and not the effect size has reported half the sentence.
  • The bottom block is the point of the page. Phones, Wi-Fi, microwaves, deodorant, plastic bottles, non-stick pans, sugar — heavily studied, consistently flat. Attention spent here is attention not spent on the top block.
  • Where you live is a row too. Air pollution, cooking smoke, arsenic in groundwater and occupational exposures are not lifestyle choices. They move with policy and infrastructure rather than with individual resolve.

How to read this, and how not to. The 0–4 strength value deliberately fuses two things — how settled the evidence is, and how large the effect is — into one axis, because a single colour can only carry one. It is an editorial simplification for this page, not a published index; the scatter panel exists to pull the two apart again. Values reflect the broad consensus of IARC monographs, World Cancer Research Fund reviews and large cohort studies, rounded hard.

Everything here is relative risk across populations. A large multiplier on a rare cancer can mean less in absolute terms than a small multiplier on a common one, and none of it describes any individual. This page cannot tell you your risk, and is not medical advice. 26 rows chosen for everyday recognisability, not for completeness — the biggest gap is occupational exposure.
